# CA-002 — Compliance-Driven Training That Reduces Performance

**Status:** Canonical Application (Non-Normative)  
**Ontology Version:** REPAC v1.0 (Immutable)  
**Purpose:** Demonstration of analytical use only  
**Boundary Note:** This document does not introduce, modify, extend, or reinterpret REPAC ontology constructs. It applies REPAC v1.0 as published.

---

## 1. Case Claim (The Misdiagnosis)

The training program failed because it was **poorly designed** or **insufficiently engaging**.

Following disappointing performance outcomes, attention focused on instructional quality: course length, interactivity, facilitator skill, and learner motivation. The dominant narrative was that better design or more engaging delivery would have produced better results.

---

## 2. Observed Outcomes (Without Interpretation)

- Mandatory training was completed at high rates.
- Learners passed required assessments.
- Compliance metrics were met or exceeded.
- Training content aligned with documented procedures.
- Post-training performance metrics stagnated or declined.
- Experienced staff increasingly relied on informal guidance.
- Supervisors observed greater hesitation and rule-checking in routine work.

Training “worked” by formal measures, yet performance suffered.

---

## 3. REPAC Domain Walkthrough

REPAC analysis examines each domain as **necessary but not sufficient**, avoiding collapse into a single explanatory cause.

---

### 3.1 Purpose

- The explicit purpose of training was regulatory compliance.
- Success was defined as completion and test scores.
- Improvement in real-world task performance was implicit but unstated.

**Observation:** Purpose prioritized defensibility over capability.

---

### 3.2 Source

- Content sources were policies, standards, and audit findings.
- Minimal input was drawn from high performers or frontline scenarios.
- Historical incidents shaped emphasis more than current work patterns.

**Observation:** Source inputs reflected institutional risk history rather than present operational reality.

---

### 3.3 Imperative

- Training was framed as mandatory and non-negotiable.
- Failure to complete training carried formal consequences.
- Little tolerance existed for deviation or contextual judgment.

**Observation:** Imperative constrained learning toward rule adherence.

---

### 3.4 Scope

- Scope was limited to documented procedures.
- Edge cases and situational trade-offs were explicitly excluded.
- Learners were instructed to escalate uncertainty rather than reason through it.

**Observation:** Scope removed the conditions under which judgment develops.

---

### 3.5 Work

- Training work emphasized content delivery and assessment completion.
- Learners optimized for passing rather than understanding.
- Practice was abstracted from real work conditions.

**Observation:** Training work reproduced compliance artifacts, not work capability.

---

### 3.6 Value

- Value was measured through completion rates and audit readiness.
- No direct linkage existed between training outcomes and performance improvement.
- Productivity loss emerged indirectly through slower decision-making.

**Observation:** Value realization assumptions conflated compliance with effectiveness.

---

### 3.7 Governance

- Training requirements were formally approved.
- Oversight ensured consistency and coverage.
- Deviations from standardized content were discouraged.

**Observation:** Governance successfully enforced uniformity.

---

### 3.8 Communication

- Messaging emphasized obligation and consequences.
- Informal conversations reframed training as “covering yourself.”
- Signals about performance impact surfaced but remained anecdotal.

**Observation:** Communication reinforced compliance framing.

---

### 3.9 Collaboration

- Training design was centralized.
- Operational teams had limited influence on content.
- Peer-to-peer learning occurred outside formal structures.

**Observation:** Collaboration compensated informally for formal rigidity.

---

## 4. Why “Poor Training Design” Is an Attractive Explanation

Attributing failure to instructional quality is appealing because it:

- Preserves the legitimacy of compliance objectives
- Suggests solvable fixes (more engaging content, better facilitation)
- Avoids questioning purpose, scope, or imperative
- Places responsibility on L&D rather than organizational constraints

However, REPAC analysis shows that training performed exactly as constrained.

---

## 5. REPAC-Compliant Explanation

Performance degradation resulted from **systemic misalignment across domains**.

Specifically:

- Purpose framed training as compliance protection.
- Source privileged policy over practice.
- Imperative discouraged judgment.
- Scope excluded situational reasoning.
- Work optimized for assessment success.
- Value was inferred from defensibility, not effectiveness.
- Governance enforced consistency.
- Communication normalized risk avoidance.
- Collaboration adapted outside the system.

Training did not fail.  
It **successfully produced compliant but less capable performers**.

---

## 6. Boundary Reflection

REPAC does not prescribe training redesigns.

This analysis does not recommend:
- More engaging modules
- Different instructional strategies
- Additional practice exercises
- Alternative assessment methods

It demonstrates that without revisiting upstream analytical commitments, improvements in training design alone cannot restore performance.

REPAC’s role is to surface how organizational learning outcomes are shaped long before instruction begins.
