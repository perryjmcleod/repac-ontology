# Scope ↔ Governance Bridge (Pilot)

This document clarifies how Scope and Governance interact as analytically distinct but mutually constraining domains within REPAC vNext. It is non-prescriptive and intended to support disciplined explanation rather than intervention design.

## 1. Analytical Distinction

- **Scope** defines boundary commitments: what is included, excluded, assumed fixed, deferred, or treated as invariant for a given analysis.
- **Governance** defines authority and control: who has the right to set, revise, enforce, or waive those boundary commitments, and under what conditions.

Scope specifies *what the boundaries are*; Governance specifies *who controls those boundaries and how*.

## 2. Boundary Authority

Governance interacts with Scope primarily through boundary authority. Boundary authority determines:
- who can establish initial Scope commitments,
- who can approve Scope changes,
- who can grant exceptions or waivers,
- and who is accountable for boundary integrity over time.

When boundary authority is unclear, fragmented, or bypassed, Scope instability should be analysed as a governance condition rather than a purely analytical or execution error.

## 3. Scope Change vs. Scope Violation

A critical analytic distinction is required between:
- **Legitimate Scope change** — a boundary adjustment that is authorised, evidenced, and discoverable under the governing ruleset; and
- **Scope violation** — a boundary deviation that occurs without recognised authority, documentation, or control.

Governance analysis determines whether a Scope deviation represents controlled adaptation or governance failure.

## 4. Exception Handling

Exceptions sit at the Scope–Governance interface.

An exception is analytically valid only when:
- the authority to grant it is defined,
- the exception is explicitly approved,
- the rationale is recorded,
- and the exception is discoverable after the fact.

Undocumented or tacit exceptions constitute governance conditions even when outcomes appear acceptable.

## 5. Control Signals and Evidence

Scope↔Governance analysis requires explicit reference to control signals, such as:
- approvals or sign-offs,
- change control records,
- policy references,
- audit trails,
- escalation or review artifacts.

Absent or ambiguous control signals weaken both Scope stability and Governance accountability, regardless of intent.

## 6. Diagnostic Use

When **Scope breakdown** is observed:
- Governance analysis asks who had authority to define or alter the boundary, and whether that authority was exercised legitimately.

When **Governance weakness** is alleged:
- Scope analysis forces the concrete question of which boundary commitments changed, how they changed, and what evidence exists.

This bidirectional check prevents single-domain explanations and supports comparable analysis across cases.

## 7. Non-Prescriptive Stance

This bridge does not specify how Scope *should* be governed.  
It provides analytic structure for explaining:
- how boundary commitments were controlled,
- how authority was exercised,
- and how deviations became possible or legitimate.

The purpose is explanatory clarity, not governance design.
